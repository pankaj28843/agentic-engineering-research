# 18. The squeezed seven-to-ten-year cohort

> **Report point:** Apprenticeship, bullet 18, page 6 of the [Thoughtworks retreat report](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/report/tw_future_of_software_engineering_europe_2026.pdf)
> **Evidence judgment:** Identity and role-transition mechanisms are plausible; unique risk for a seven-to-ten-year cohort is unsupported.

The retreat surfaced an intuitively powerful story. Engineers with roughly seven to ten years of experience may have invested deeply in implementation expertise just as agents make parts of that expertise cheaper. They may lack the institutional authority of senior leaders while being expected to transform faster than juniors whose professional identity is less settled.

The story may describe some people accurately. It is not a demographic finding. No audited source defines that cohort in advance, counts exposed and comparison groups, measures identity strain with a validated instrument, or separates AI from layoffs, hiring contraction, remote work, management quality, and changing role expectations. Tenure is a clue to investigate, not a basis for policy.

## A plain-language model: exposure, meaning, and support

Role transition pressure can be pictured as four interacting questions:

- **Exposure:** Which valued tasks are shrinking, changing, or moving to agents?
- **Meaning:** How central were those tasks to the person’s identity, status, and sense of competence?
- **Practice opportunity:** What new decisions can the person attempt often enough to become skilled?
- **Support and conditions:** Are time, coaching, authority, psychological safety, and credible career paths available amid broader labor-market pressure?

Years of experience can correlate with these factors, but it does not determine them. A ten-year engineer who already leads domain discovery may gain leverage. A senior specialist whose status rests on a narrow implementation craft may feel severe devaluation. A junior may lose the routine work through which confidence would have formed. Two people with identical tenure can face opposite transitions because their tasks, organization, and prior tool exposure differ.

This model treats identity strain as an interaction between work design and personal meaning. It does not diagnose a psychological condition or assume resistance is an individual defect.

## What the evidence can—and cannot—say

The [Microsoft New Future of Work Report 2025](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/New-Future-Of-Work-Report-2025.pdf) is useful as a secondary map of a heterogeneous evidence base. It summarizes labor, hiring, skill, trust, learning, and teaming research. Some underlying working papers concern an estimated relative employment decline among ages 22–25 in highly exposed occupations and reduced junior hiring after firm AI adoption. Those are not direct findings about software engineers with seven to ten years’ experience. Their population, study status, identification strategy, and confounding limits must travel with any use.

The report therefore supports a broader point: labor and skill effects can differ by role and population, and individual productivity does not automatically aggregate into healthy careers or teams. It does not establish a mid-career identity effect or a suitable support program.

[AI Deskilling Is a Structural Problem](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02686-z) adds a conceptual lens. The peer-reviewed philosophical paper describes “capacity-hostile environments” in which systems, incentives, division of work, shared valuation, and reduced opportunities to practise can undermine agency. It also allows upskilling and deskilling to coexist. Because it reports no software workplace study, effect size, prevalence, or longitudinal result, it should be used to ask structural questions rather than to declare that a cohort has been deskilled.

A [CHI survey of 319 knowledge workers](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) collected 936 first-hand examples from people who used generative AI at least weekly for work. Participants reported different critical-thinking effort depending on confidence in the AI and in themselves. The sample was adoption-selected, English-speaking, technology-skewed, and young: 71.79% were 18–34. Perceived effort is not objective skill, retention, or identity strain, and age is not tenure. The survey gives variables worth examining but cannot identify the proposed cohort.

Retreat sources own the hypothesis. Fowler’s [13 February fragment](https://martinfowler.com/fragments/2026-02-13.html) records concerns about junior and “mid-level” engineers, burnout, context switching, and cognitive debt. [Andrew Harmel-Law](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/software-engineering-having-raft-moment-andrew-harmel-law-f6mle/) writes thoughtfully about professional identity and practices as containers for reasoning. [Yanina Ledovaya](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/my-future-software-engineering-retreat-psychological-notes-ledovaya-vr87e/) reports vocabulary gaps and cognitive-exhaustion impressions through a psychology lens.

These are firsthand reflections from an overlapping retreat network, not workforce studies. They provide attribution, mechanisms, and research questions. Repetition does not supply a denominator, validated measure, or independent replication. Ledovaya’s expertise sharpens the questions; it does not turn retreat observations into diagnoses.

## Competing explanations are not footnotes

A fair inquiry must test causes outside AI use. A [Hacker News discussion about remote work and junior development](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48326721) is not evidence for any cause, but it usefully lists hypotheses: remote mentoring, post-ZIRP hiring, layoffs, internships, management, tax treatment, and investment incentives. Self-selected comments cannot rank those explanations. They do show why a single-technology story is underidentified.

Task allocation may matter more than tenure. If organizations automate routine implementation while giving experienced engineers no corresponding authority over product, architecture, or operations, some people can be left with review burden but little creative control. Alternatively, agents may remove tedious work and open a path into broader design. Prior exposure, codebase familiarity, domain knowledge, disability and accessibility, caregiving constraints, and local support can all alter that experience.

Labor conditions can also turn ordinary learning uncertainty into identity threat. If headcount is contracting while leaders describe AI as a replacement, a training invitation may feel like an employment screen. The same intervention can be experienced as investment or surveillance depending on decision rights and trust.

These alternatives weaken the claim that a tenure band is uniquely exposed. They strengthen the case for listening at the level of role, task, and person.

There are counterexamples in both directions. Mid-career engineers may use agents to escape repetitive maintenance and gain space for domain or leadership work. Junior people may face the sharper loss if entry-level tasks disappear, while very senior specialists may experience devaluation when a rare implementation skill becomes easy to imitate. These are plausible patterns, not findings in this corpus. Their value is to stop a single cohort story from organizing the evidence before the organization has listened.

## Exercise: a cohort-free transition audit

Run confidential listening sessions organized by work, not years served. Include people who create, review, operate, support, and manage the same system.

Ask each participant:

1. Which tasks have changed, disappeared, or grown since agent use expanded?
2. Which change increased or reduced their sense of useful contribution?
3. Which new decisions are they expected to make, and where can they practise them safely?
4. What support, authority, or career signal is missing?
5. Which non-AI change—staffing, remote work, reorganization, product pressure—could explain the same experience?

Aggregate themes only when group size protects confidentiality. Do not label an individual “at risk,” infer capacity from tenure, or route responses into performance management. Compare expressed needs across roles and conditions; offer coaching, practice time, peer learning, and role redesign through opt-in or broadly available routes. Revisit after changes to see whether the work environment, not just sentiment, improved.

## What would establish unique cohort risk

A credible study would pre-register tenure and role groups, measure task exposure and prior AI use, use a validated identity or well-being outcome, and follow people over time. It would compare organizational support, labor conditions, role changes, and opportunity to practise, while preserving worker voice and examining attrition. A finding would need to show that the tenure band predicts outcomes beyond those rival explanations.

The analysis should also test heterogeneous effects instead of forcing one average story. It should report who gains new authority, who absorbs review or cleanup work, and who exits before follow-up. Confidential qualitative accounts can explain why the same task shift feels like expansion to one person and devaluation to another, but they should complement rather than substitute for denominators and comparisons.

Until then, “seven to ten years” should remain a quoted retreat hypothesis, not a segmentation rule.

## What survives skeptical review

Agentic change can plausibly unsettle professional identity by altering valued tasks, status, practice opportunities, and authority. Structural conditions can make that transition supportive or capacity-hostile. The audited evidence does not show that mid-career engineers are uniquely affected or that tenure identifies who needs help.

Treat strain as information about work design. Offer support by expressed need and actual conditions, not by a demographic story that could stigmatize the people it means to protect.

Podcast hook: What if the “squeezed middle” is not a cohort at all, but a pattern created wherever responsibility grows while authority, practice, and career meaning shrink?
